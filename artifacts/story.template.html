01 The discovery

A method that reads the past out of the present

On 30 July 2026 a team at UC Berkeley reported that every living human carries DNA from at least two extinct populations that have never been sequenced — and they found them without a single fragment of ancient DNA.

The technique is called TRACE — TRacking Archaic Contributions via ARG Estimation. It reconstructs an ancestral recombination graph: a running record, across the whole genome, of how every stretch of DNA in a sample is related to every other and how far back those relationships go. Read that graph carefully and two lineages fall out that match nothing anyone has ever sequenced.

One is in everyone, which puts the interbreeding inside Africa. The other never met us at all — it interbred with Denisovans, and Denisovans passed it on.

Everything below rebuilds that reasoning from first principles on the ruvstack, against a simulated cohort where the truth is known, and then pushes it until it breaks.

02 Deep time

Four archaic sources, two of them invisible

To know whether a detector works you need a world where you already know the answer. The cohort is simulated under a structured coalescent on a hominin population tree calibrated to the published divergence times, with interbreeding planted at known coordinates.

03 The platform

What is actually running underneath

Nothing here is a notebook full of Python. The whole study is a Rust binary that compiles to native code or WebAssembly and runs the same on a laptop, a browser tab, or a Raspberry Pi on a shelf. That property is the reason the stack looks the way it does.

How a question moves through it

Read that left to right: raw sequence becomes a numeric fingerprint, the fingerprint goes into a searchable index, the index narrows millions of possible comparisons down to a handful, the exact genetics runs only on those, and two feedback loops tune the whole thing without a human in the way.

04 The engine

Making a quadratic question cheap

Asking "who is this segment related to?" the obvious way means comparing it with every other segment, in every window. That is quadratic, and almost all of the work is wasted on haplotypes that were never going to matter.

So the pipeline splits the question in two. Every window is turned into a k-mer profile vector — a numeric fingerprint of which short sequences it contains — and inserted into a RuVector HNSW graph. The index answers which segments are even worth looking at. The exact genetics, which decides the answer, runs only on what comes back.

05 Darwin & the flywheel

Two loops around a frozen model

A detector like this has eight dials. Hand-tuning them is guesswork, and guesswork that touches the thresholds is guesswork that decides the answer. Darwin mode does it mechanically: change one dial, test the change in a sandbox against a training split, keep it only if it clears an explicit margin. Then score the survivor once on a split it has never seen.

The flywheel, and the bug that nearly ate it

The flywheel is the outer loop: confirmed ghost segments are condensed into references and written back into the index, so the next pass can recognise a borderline segment by its resemblance to a lineage that is no longer invisible.

The first version collapsed. It let any ghost call become a reference, so false positives validated more false positives and precision fell from 0.84 to 0.19 over three rounds. Two rules fixed it, and both come from the population genetics rather than from tuning.

06 What came out

Two lineages, and no reference genome for either

Every segment flagged as archaic that could not be matched to a Neanderthal or Denisovan reference is a ghost candidate. Clustering those by coalescent depth finds two modes that bracket the two planted lineages.

They do not separate cleanly. Ghost-A's own depth distribution runs to 2.5 million years, so its deep tail lands on top of Ghost-B, and with only super-archaic segments depth can tell you two sources are present but cannot assign individual segments between them.

A second signature, independent of depth

Depth says when a lineage split. It says nothing about how its DNA reached us — and that is exactly where the two ghosts differ. Ghost-A entered our lineage inside Africa, before the last migration out, so it should be everywhere. Ghost-B never met us: it came through Denisovans, so it should pile up wherever Denisovan ancestry piles up.

07 Pushing it until it breaks

Three things the first run could not tell us

Getting a good answer on one cohort is worth very little on its own. These three sweeps ask the harder question: where does this stop working, and why?

How rare can a ghost lineage get?

A lineage is only recoverable as a lineage once enough of its segments survive to form a mode of its own. Below that it is swallowed by the deep tail of the more common ghost. So we varied the super-archaic contribution and watched for the moment it disappears.

How much DNA does one window need?

Coalescent depth is estimated by counting the differences between two stretches of sequence. Too few differences and the estimate is noise. This sweep shrinks the window until the method fails, which gives the real resolution limit in the only unit that matters — the number of variable sites available.

Is the shortcut lying to us?

The pipeline lets an approximate index decide which comparisons to run. That is a shortcut, and shortcuts have to be audited rather than assumed. So we ran the detector twice on the same cohort — once with the index, once comparing everything against everything — and diffed the calls.
