01 The discovery

A method that reads the past out of the present

On 30 July 2026 a team at UC Berkeley reported that every living human carries DNA from at least two extinct populations that have never been sequenced — and they found them without a single fragment of ancient DNA.

The technique is called TRACE — TRacking Archaic Contributions via ARG Estimation. It reconstructs an ancestral recombination graph: a running record, across the whole genome, of how every stretch of DNA in a sample is related to every other and how far back those relationships go. Read that graph carefully and two lineages fall out that match nothing anyone has ever sequenced.

One is in everyone, which puts the interbreeding inside Africa. The other never met us at all \u2014 it interbred with Denisovans, and Denisovans passed it on.

What it beats, and what beats it

TRACE was benchmarked against three existing methods \u2014 hmmix, Sprime and IBDmix. It is not uniformly better, and the shape of the trade is the interesting part.

Two things stand out. IBDmix finds far more Neanderthal DNA than TRACE does \u2014 78% recall against 53% \u2014 and the authors say so plainly: their method recovers less total archaic ancestry than previous estimates. But IBDmix is almost useless on Denisovans here (17.6% precision), and neither competitor can represent a ghost at all, because both need an unadmixed outgroup to compare against and a ghost that introgressed before the split contaminates the outgroup too.

That is the actual claim. Not \u201cbetter\u201d \u2014 able to see a category of thing the others are structurally blind to, at the cost of missing more of what they can see. On a null model with no ghost at all, TRACE infers 0.07% ghost ancestry (95% CI 0\u20130.3%), against the 0.49\u20131.1% it reports on real data.

Ghost ancestry piles up in immune and metabolic regions \u2014 the MHC and lipoprotein complexes, p < 10\u207b\u2075 \u2014 with high-frequency peaks at CSMD1 and RBFOX1. The super-archaic fraction in Oceanians concentrates in RNF39, PPP1R11, POLR1H (all MHC) and CYP24A1, which regulates vitamin D breakdown.

Everything below rebuilds that reasoning from first principles on the ruvstack, against a simulated cohort where the truth is known, and then pushes it until it breaks.

02 Real DNA

The same question, asked of real people

Everything else here is validated against a simulation where the answer is known. This section takes the statistic that validation licenses and points it at real human genomes.

800 phased haplotypes from 400 people in the 1000 Genomes Project, 80 from each of the five superpopulations, across a 1 Mb stretch of chromosome 22 carrying real variable sites. Pulled straight out of the bgzipped VCF over HTTP range requests — no whole-genome download, no reference panel of our own, nothing simulated.

The result that had to come out right

Before believing anything a method says about ghosts, it has to reproduce something already known. The oldest and firmest fact in human population genetics is that African genomes carry more variation than everyone else's — the non-African populations all descend from one small group that left, so their lineages meet each other much more recently.

The deep tail, at real coordinates

These are the segments in this region that must reach furthest back before they meet anything else in the panel. They are real haplotypes at real positions on chromosome 22, and you could go and look them up.

Three sequenced archaics, over the same coordinates

A deep segment is an outlier, not an attribution — it says a stretch of DNA is old, not where it came from. Answering the second question needs something to compare against, so the same range of chromosome 22 was pulled from three sequenced archaic genomes: the Altai Neanderthal (~52× coverage), Vindija 33.19 (~30×) and Denisova 3 (~30×), from the Max Planck Institute's snpAD call set.

The obvious thing to do is measure how different each living person is from the Neanderthal, and check whether non-Africans come out closer. That does not work, and it is worth knowing why, because the reason catches people out.

Every genome in this comparison — including the Neanderthal's — was written down as a list of differences from one agreed-on template sequence. That template was assembled mostly from people of European descent. So African DNA differs from it more, everywhere, for reasons that have nothing to do with Neanderthals. Run the comparison anyway and you get a number that leans the right way and means nothing.

There is a test that gets around it, and it is simple enough to describe in one sentence. Take one African person's DNA and one non-African person's DNA. Find a spot where they disagree. Look up what the Neanderthal has there. It matches one of them. Do that a few million times and count.

If nobody ever interbred, the Neanderthal is equally related to both, so it should side with each of them about half the time. Any lean is interbreeding. The reason this beats the divergence comparison is that both outcomes require the Neanderthal to carry the same kind of allele, so whatever bias the reference introduces is applied to both sides of the count and drops out. Geneticists call the two outcomes ABBA and BABA, and the leftover lean Patterson's D — it has been the standard test for archaic interbreeding since the first Neanderthal genome in 2010.

02 Deep time

Four archaic sources, two of them invisible

To know whether a detector works you need a world where you already know the answer. The cohort is simulated under a structured coalescent on a hominin population tree calibrated to the published divergence times, with interbreeding planted at known coordinates.

03 The platform

What is actually running underneath

Nothing here is a notebook full of Python. The whole study is a Rust binary that compiles to native code or WebAssembly and runs the same on a laptop, a browser tab, or a Raspberry Pi on a shelf. That property is the reason the stack looks the way it does.

How a question moves through it

Read that left to right: raw sequence becomes a numeric fingerprint, the fingerprint goes into a searchable index, the index narrows millions of possible comparisons down to a handful, the exact genetics runs only on those, and two feedback loops tune the whole thing without a human in the way.

04 The engine

Making a quadratic question cheap

Asking "who is this segment related to?" the obvious way means comparing it with every other segment, in every window. That is quadratic, and almost all of the work is wasted on haplotypes that were never going to matter.

So the pipeline splits the question in two. Every window is turned into a k-mer profile vector — a numeric fingerprint of which short sequences it contains — and inserted into a RuVector HNSW graph. The index answers which segments are even worth looking at. The exact genetics, which decides the answer, runs only on what comes back.

05 Darwin & the flywheel

Two loops around a frozen model

A detector like this has eight dials. Hand-tuning them is guesswork, and guesswork that touches the thresholds is guesswork that decides the answer. Darwin mode does it mechanically: change one dial, test the change in a sandbox against a training split, keep it only if it clears an explicit margin. Then score the survivor once on a split it has never seen.

The flywheel, and the bug that nearly ate it

The flywheel is the outer loop: confirmed ghost segments are condensed into references and written back into the index, so the next pass can recognise a borderline segment by its resemblance to a lineage that is no longer invisible.

The first version collapsed. It let any ghost call become a reference, so false positives validated more false positives and precision fell from 0.84 to 0.19 over three rounds. Two rules fixed it, and both come from the population genetics rather than from tuning.

06 What came out

Two lineages, and no reference genome for either

Every segment flagged as archaic that could not be matched to a Neanderthal or Denisovan reference is a ghost candidate. Clustering those by coalescent depth finds two modes that bracket the two planted lineages.

They do not separate cleanly. Ghost-A's own depth distribution runs to 2.5 million years, so its deep tail lands on top of Ghost-B, and with only super-archaic segments depth can tell you two sources are present but cannot assign individual segments between them.

A second signature, independent of depth

Depth says when a lineage split. It says nothing about how its DNA reached us — and that is exactly where the two ghosts differ. Ghost-A entered our lineage inside Africa, before the last migration out, so it should be everywhere. Ghost-B never met us: it came through Denisovans, so it should pile up wherever Denisovan ancestry piles up.

07 Pushing it until it breaks

Three things the first run could not tell us

Getting a good answer on one cohort is worth very little on its own. These three sweeps ask the harder question: where does this stop working, and why?

How rare can a ghost lineage get?

A lineage is only recoverable as a lineage once enough of its segments survive to form a mode of its own. Below that it is swallowed by the deep tail of the more common ghost. So we varied the super-archaic contribution and watched for the moment it disappears.

How much DNA does one window need?

Coalescent depth is estimated by counting the differences between two stretches of sequence. Too few differences and the estimate is noise. This sweep shrinks the window until the method fails, which gives the real resolution limit in the only unit that matters — the number of variable sites available.

Is the shortcut lying to us?

The pipeline lets an approximate index decide which comparisons to run. That is a shortcut, and shortcuts have to be audited rather than assumed. So we ran the detector twice on the same cohort — once with the index, once comparing everything against everything — and diffed the calls.

Does looking sideways along the chromosome help?

Everything above judges each window on its own. But introgression does not arrive one window at a time — it arrives in tracts, contiguous runs of chromosome inherited whole and then chopped up by recombination over the generations since. So a window that is borderline on its own evidence, sitting between two windows that are clearly archaic, is more likely archaic than the same window sitting alone.

The cohort was re-simulated with introgression planted as geometric tracts of mean 4.9 windows, and a linkage-aware rule added: call a window if its own depth clears the threshold, or if it clears a lower rescue threshold and its neighbours' decay-weighted depth clears a context threshold.

Which genomes should you actually sequence?

The detector never looks at an archaic genome to decide whether a segment is archaic — it only compares against living people. So the reference panel is the budget item that matters, and the sweep asks two questions a real study has to answer: how many genomes, and whose?

Corrections

Earlier versions of this page repeated the press-release claim that “Denisovans carry 3–5% super-archaic DNA”. That figure is not in the paper. TRACE's estimate is 0.3% of the Denisovan ancestry in Oceanians — a different denominator entirely, and an explicit lower bound. The same versions described 800 ka as the ghost's divergence time; it is a coalescence time, which bounds the divergence from above. The authors' own conservative statement is “diverged over 500,000 years ago”.
