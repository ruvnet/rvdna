00Deep analysis · rvDNA · RuVector · metaharness

Two ancestors nobody
has ever seen

On 30 July 2026, a team at UC Berkeley reported that every living human carries DNA from at least two extinct hominin populations that have never been sequenced, never been dug up, and have no name beyond the shape they leave in our genealogies.

They found them without a single fragment of ancient DNA. The evidence came entirely from the genomes of people alive today — from a property of those genomes that is easy to state and hard to measure: some stretches of our DNA are far older than they have any right to be.

What follows is that logic, rebuilt from first principles on the rvDNA engine: a coalescent simulator to generate a cohort with known truth, a RuVector HNSW index to make the genealogy search tractable, a Darwin-mode loop to tune the detector against itself, and a flywheel that turns each confirmed ghost segment into evidence for finding the next one.

01What was reported

A method that reads the past out of the present

The technique is called TRACE — TRacking Archaic Contributions via ARG Estimation. It reconstructs, across the whole genome, an ancestral recombination graph: a running record of how every stretch of DNA in a sample is related to every other, and how far back those relationships go.

Read that graph carefully and two lineages fall out that match nothing we have ever sequenced.

The first is in everyone — African and non-African alike — which places the interbreeding inside Africa, before the last migration out of it. The second never met us directly at all: it interbred with Denisovans, and Denisovans passed it on.

Both sit in a genome that is already about 2% archaic. What makes them different from the Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry we have known about for years is that there is nothing to compare them to. No bone, no genome, no reference. They are defined entirely by absence.

02Why a genealogy gives it away

The only thing you need to measure is depth

Take any window of the genome and ask, of one person's copy: how far back do I have to go before this stretch of DNA meets anybody else's?

For almost every window the answer is recent. You share that stretch with a cousin, a population, a continent. But if the window came into your ancestry from a lineage that split off 800,000 years ago, it cannot meet anyone until it gets back past that split — because until then, there was nobody else to meet.

Note what the lower edge of each distribution is doing. A segment's coalescent depth is always older than the split that produced it, because the donor lineage had its own history to get through first — Ghost-A's segments were planted at 800 ka and their median depth is 980 ka. In a clean sample the bottom of the range would therefore be the honest divergence-time estimator. In the recovered clusters further down it is not, because false positives collect at the bottom and drag it low. Both numbers are reported there, and neither is treated as precision.
03The demography under the simulation

Four archaic sources, two of them invisible

To know whether a detector works you need a world where you already know the answer. The cohort here is simulated under a structured coalescent on a hominin population tree calibrated to the published divergence times, with introgression planted at known coordinates.

04rvDNA · RuVector HNSW

Making a quadratic question cheap

Asking "who is this segment related to?" naively means comparing it with every other segment in the panel, in every window. That is quadratic, and most of the comparisons are wasted on haplotypes that were never going to be relevant.

So the pipeline splits the question in two. Every window of every haplotype is embedded as an rvDNA k-mer profile vector and inserted into a RuVector HNSW graph. The index answers which segments are even worth looking at; exact, Jukes–Cantor-corrected divergence — the part that actually decides the answer — runs only against those.

05metaharness · Darwin mode

The detector tunes itself, under a gate

A detector like this has eight knobs — k-mer size, vector width, graph connectivity, search breadth, how many neighbours to retrieve, and three depth thresholds. Hand-tuning them is guesswork, and guesswork that touches the thresholds is guesswork that decides the answer.

Darwin mode does it mechanically. Mutate one hyperparameter, evaluate the mutant in a sandbox against a training split only, and keep the change only if it clears an explicit margin — so evaluation noise cannot ratchet the parameters anywhere. Then score the survivor once on a held-out split it never saw, which is where overfitting would show up if there were any.

06metaharness · Flywheel

Each confirmed ghost makes the next one easier

In the first pass a ghost segment can only be found the hard way: by proving its genealogy is an outlier against the entire living panel. That works, but it misses the borderline cases — the segments whose depth is real but not extreme enough to clear a threshold set conservatively enough to avoid false positives.

The flywheel closes that gap. Confirmed ghost segments are condensed into consensus references and written back into the vector index. On the next turn a borderline segment can be recognised by its resemblance to a lineage that is no longer invisible — and the loop stops itself when a turn adds nothing new.

07The result

Two lineages, and no reference genome for either

Every segment the pipeline flagged as archaic but could not match a Neanderthal or Denisovan reference is a ghost candidate. Clustering those by coalescent depth finds two modes, and the two modes bracket the two planted lineages.

They do not separate cleanly, and the reason is worth stating plainly. Ghost-A's own depth distribution runs all the way to 2.5 Ma, so its deep tail lands on top of Ghost-B. With only super-archaic segments in the whole cohort to work with, depth cannot pull them apart segment by segment — it can only tell you that two sources are present.

A second, independent signature

Depth says when a lineage split. It says nothing about how its DNA reached us — and that is exactly where the two ghosts differ. Ghost-A entered the modern human lineage inside Africa, before the last migration out, so it should appear everywhere. Ghost-B never met us at all: it reached modern genomes through Denisovans, so it should concentrate wherever Denisovan ancestry concentrates.

That prediction is testable without touching the depth axis, and the recovered clusters honour it.
